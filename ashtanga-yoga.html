Ashtanga Yoga

The Eight-Limbed Path of Patanjali

Yoga is much more than physical postures. The classical Ashtanga framework presents Yoga as a complete path involving conduct, personal discipline, posture, breath, attention, meditation and deeper states of awareness.

What Is Ashtanga Yoga?

The word Ashtanga comes from two Sanskrit words: ashta, meaning "eight", and anga, meaning "limb" or "part".

In this page, Ashtanga Yoga means the eight-limbed Yoga framework associated with Patanjali's Yoga Sutras.

These eight limbs are:

  1. Yama — ethical principles for our relationship with others
  2. Niyama — personal observances and self-discipline
  3. Asana — steady and suitable posture
  4. Pranayama — conscious regulation of breath
  5. Pratyahara — turning attention inward from sensory distractions
  6. Dharana — concentration
  7. Dhyana — meditation
  8. Samadhi — deep meditative absorption

An important point for beginners

Many people today use the word "Yoga" mainly for physical postures. Asana is certainly an important part of Yoga, but in Patanjali's eight-limbed framework it is only one of eight limbs.

This wider view helps us understand Yoga as a way of developing the whole person — conduct, body, breath, attention and inner awareness.

A Useful Distinction

Classical Ashtanga Yoga and Modern Ashtanga Practice

The expression Ashtanga Yoga can create confusion because it is used in more than one way today.

In classical Yoga study, Ashtanga refers to Patanjali's eight-limbed framework.

In modern Yoga, the term can also refer to a particular physical practice commonly called Ashtanga Vinyasa Yoga.

Classical Ashtanga

The eight-limbed path of Patanjali: Yama, Niyama, Asana, Pranayama, Pratyahara, Dharana, Dhyana and Samadhi.

Modern Ashtanga Vinyasa

A modern physical Yoga tradition known for structured sequences of postures connected with breath.

On this page, our main subject is the classical eight-limbed path.

The Complete Framework

The Eight Limbs of Yoga

Let us understand each limb in simple language.

Limb 1

Yama — How We Relate to Others

Yama can be understood as a group of ethical principles or restraints that guide our behaviour toward other people and the world around us.

Patanjali describes five Yamas.

1. Ahimsa — Non-Harming

Simple meaning: Try not to cause unnecessary harm.

Ahimsa can involve our actions, words and attitudes.

Example: If another person makes a mistake, we can correct them without insulting or humiliating them.

2. Satya — Truthfulness

Simple meaning: Be truthful and sincere.

Truthfulness should also be guided by responsibility and kindness.

Example: A teacher can honestly tell a student that more practice is needed without making the student feel ashamed.

3. Asteya — Non-Stealing

Simple meaning: Do not take what has not been given to you.

The idea can also encourage respect for another person's time, effort, ideas and belongings.

Example: Give proper credit when using another person's work or idea.

4. Brahmacharya — Responsible Use of Energy

Simple meaning: Use your energy wisely and responsibly.

Interpretations of Brahmacharya vary across Yoga traditions and contexts. It should not be reduced to one simple modern definition.

Example: A student learns to balance study, work, recreation, rest and Yoga practice rather than exhausting themselves.

5. Aparigraha — Non-Grasping

Simple meaning: Do not become unnecessarily attached to possessions or constant accumulation.

Example: Instead of always wanting the newest things, appreciate what you already have and use it responsibly.

Limb 2

Niyama — How We Develop Ourselves

Niyama refers to personal observances and disciplines. They help a practitioner develop cleanliness, contentment, discipline, self-study and a deeper relationship with their chosen spiritual or philosophical ideal.

1. Shaucha — Cleanliness

Simple meaning: Keep the body, surroundings and practice space clean.

Example: Keeping your Yoga mat and practice area clean and orderly.

2. Santosha — Contentment

Simple meaning: Learn to appreciate the present without constant dissatisfaction.

Example: A beginner does not compare their posture with an advanced practitioner's posture. They appreciate their own progress.

3. Tapas — Discipline

Simple meaning: Develop steady effort and discipline.

Tapas does not mean harming or punishing yourself. It can mean maintaining sincere and appropriate effort.

Example: Practising regularly even when motivation is not very high, while still respecting the body's limits.

4. Svadhyaya — Self-Study

Simple meaning: Study yourself and learn from authentic teachings.

Example: After practice, notice how your mind behaved: Was it calm, distracted, impatient or attentive?

5. Ishvara Pranidhana — Dedication or Surrender

Simple meaning: Offer one's effort to a higher ideal or Ishvara, according to one's tradition and understanding.

Interpretations differ among Yoga traditions.

Example: Practise sincerely without making personal achievement the only purpose of the practice.

Limb 3

Asana — A Steady and Suitable Posture

Asana is commonly understood today as Yoga posture. In Patanjali's framework, however, the concept is closely connected with establishing a stable and comfortable basis for practice.

The deeper lesson is not simply: "How difficult is the pose?"

A better question is: "Can I remain steady, attentive and appropriately comfortable?"

Simple example

A beginner may sit comfortably on a chair for meditation. Another person may sit cross-legged on the floor. The goal is not to impress someone with a difficult pose. The goal is to create a suitable foundation for practice.

To explore Yoga postures and the wider Yoga system, visit our Learn Yoga page.

Limb 4

Pranayama — Conscious Regulation of Breath

Pranayama is the fourth limb. It involves conscious work with breathing and is given an important place in many Yoga traditions.

Breath connects our physical practice with our attention. A quiet and appropriate breathing practice can also become a bridge toward meditation.

Simple example

Instead of immediately reacting when you feel restless, pause and observe your breathing. Allow the breath to become calm and natural before returning to the activity.

For a much deeper study of Yoga breathing, see our Pranayama page.

Advanced breath retention and intensive breathing techniques should be learned responsibly and, where appropriate, under qualified guidance.

Limb 5

Pratyahara — Turning Attention Inward

Pratyahara is often described as withdrawal of the senses. For a beginner, this phrase can sound difficult.

A simple way to understand it is: learning not to be controlled by every sensory attraction or distraction.

Everyday example

You are studying when your phone receives a notification. Your eyes want to look at the screen immediately. Instead, you notice the urge and calmly continue studying.

This does not mean that you hate your senses. It means you are learning to guide your attention.

Limb 6

Dharana — Concentration

Dharana means holding the mind or attention on a chosen object, point or subject.

The mind naturally moves from one thought to another. Dharana is the practice of bringing it back to one chosen focus.

Simple example

Sit quietly and observe your breathing. Your mind wanders to work, family, food or tomorrow's plans. You notice this and gently bring your attention back to the breath.

That returning of attention is an important part of concentration practice.

Limb 7

Dhyana — Meditation

Dhyana is commonly translated as meditation. It refers to a sustained flow of attention toward the chosen object of meditation.

Dharana and Dhyana are closely connected. Dharana emphasizes gathering and holding attention. Dhyana describes a more continuous meditative flow.

Simple example

Imagine watching the flame of a lamp. At first, your attention repeatedly moves away. With practice, attention becomes steadier and the meditation becomes more continuous.

Learn more about meditation through our Meditation page and our deeper Dhyana study page.

Limb 8

Samadhi — Deep Meditative Absorption

Samadhi is the eighth limb and one of the most profound concepts in classical Yoga philosophy.

It is traditionally described in terms of deep meditative absorption and transformation of the relationship between the practitioner, the process of meditation and the object of meditation.

Different Yoga and philosophical traditions explain Samadhi in different ways. Therefore, it is better to study the classical sources carefully rather than reducing Samadhi to a modern slogan such as "complete relaxation."

For beginners

You do not need to claim or chase an extraordinary experience to begin studying Yoga.

Start with sincere practice, ethical living, appropriate posture, breath awareness, concentration and meditation.

Yoga in Daily Life

How Can We Practise the Eight Limbs Outside the Yoga Mat?

At Home

Speak respectfully, keep your surroundings orderly, practise contentment and make time for quiet reflection.

At School

Be honest, respect other students, concentrate on your work and learn to pause before reacting.

At Work

Respect colleagues, give proper credit, manage your energy, practise focused attention and avoid unnecessary conflict.

During Yoga Practice

Respect your body, breathe appropriately, avoid competition, remain attentive and finish the practice with quiet awareness.

Understanding the Framework

Are the Eight Limbs Just Eight Steps?

It is useful to study the eight limbs in their traditional order, but beginners should not think of them as eight boxes that must be completely finished one after another.

Ethical living can support meditation. Meditation can improve awareness of behaviour. Breath practice can support concentration. Concentration can support meditation. Physical practice can prepare the body for quieter practices.

In this sense, the limbs can be understood as interconnected parts of a complete Yoga path.

A simple picture

Think of a tree.

Its roots, trunk, branches and leaves are different, but they belong to the same living system.

In the same way, the eight limbs have distinct meanings, while together they form a broader Yoga framework.

Yoga Panorama

Ashtanga Yoga and Hatha Yoga

Ashtanga Yoga and Hatha Yoga should not simply be treated as two names for exactly the same system.

Patanjali's eight-limbed framework and the later Hatha Yoga traditions developed through different textual and historical contexts. At the same time, there are important areas of connection, especially in practices involving Asana, Pranayama, meditation and disciplined living.

Our Yoga Philosophy page explores Hatha Yoga, Prana, Nadis, Chakras, Kundalini, Mudra, Bandha, Dhyana and related traditions in greater depth.

Beginner's Guide

Where Should a Beginner Start?

You do not need to understand every Sanskrit term before beginning Yoga.

Step 1 — Learn

Understand the basic ideas of Yoga and begin with simple, comfortable practices.

Step 2 — Observe

Notice your posture, breathing, thoughts and reactions without judging yourself.

Step 3 — Practise

Build a regular and appropriate practice rather than trying to do everything at once.

Step 4 — Reflect

Ask what your practice is teaching you about attention, behaviour and daily life.

Yoga for Everyone

Ashtanga Yoga for Different Ages

Yoga learning can be adapted to different ages, abilities, backgrounds and levels of experience.

Children

Simple ideas such as kindness, truthfulness, attention, breathing awareness and gentle movement can be introduced through age-appropriate activities.

Young People

Concentration, responsible habits, emotional awareness and healthy routines can become useful areas of study.

Adults

Adults can explore ethical living, physical practice, breath awareness, concentration and meditation according to their needs and experience.

Older Adults

Appropriate movement, comfortable breathing practices, attention training and meditation may be adapted to individual ability.

Important: Yoga practices should be adapted to the individual. People with medical concerns, injuries or special limitations should seek appropriate professional guidance before undertaking demanding practices.

For Yoga Teachers

Why Should a Yoga Teacher Understand All Eight Limbs?

A teacher who knows only physical postures may be able to demonstrate an asana, but a deeper understanding of Yoga helps the teacher explain why practice matters beyond movement.

Teach With Context

Explain where Asana fits within the wider Yoga tradition.

Teach Responsibly

Avoid presenting difficult practices as competitions or promising unrealistic results.

Teach Different Learners

A good teacher can adapt language, movement and practice to different ages and levels.

Encourage Study

Teachers should continue studying Yoga texts, traditions, philosophy, teaching methods and responsible modern practice.

Study & Scholarship

Classical Sources and Further Study

Serious study of Ashtanga Yoga should eventually move beyond short social-media explanations.

Important areas of study include the Yoga Sutras of Patanjali, related Indian philosophical traditions, the Bhagavad Gita and later Yoga literature.

Students should remember that translations and interpretations can differ. Reading more than one reliable translation can help develop a better understanding.

A good study habit

First learn the simple meaning. Then study the Sanskrit term. Then read the classical source. Finally, compare different interpretations.

Learn Clearly

Common Misunderstandings About Ashtanga Yoga

"Ashtanga means difficult postures."

Not in the classical sense. Ashtanga means the eight-limbed framework. Asana is only one of the eight limbs.

"Yoga is only exercise."

Physical practice is important, but the classical Yoga traditions include ethics, breath, concentration, meditation and philosophical study.

"Meditation means stopping every thought."

Meditation is not simply a fight against thoughts. Classical meditation involves the cultivation of sustained and stable attention.

"Advanced Yoga means doing extreme things."

Difficulty alone does not make a practice spiritually or philosophically advanced. Understanding, discipline, awareness and responsible practice matter greatly.

Responsible Learning

Practise With Awareness

Yoga is a discipline of learning, not a competition.

Do not force a posture simply because someone else can perform it. Do not force the breath. Do not practise advanced breath retention, intense cleansing practices, strong bandhas or other demanding techniques without suitable instruction.

Traditional Yoga contains many advanced practices. They deserve careful study and appropriate guidance.

Our approach

We aim to present traditional Yoga with respect while also explaining it in language that modern learners can understand.

Traditional beliefs and philosophical concepts should not automatically be presented as established scientific facts. Where appropriate, we distinguish traditional Yoga teachings from modern scientific explanations.
