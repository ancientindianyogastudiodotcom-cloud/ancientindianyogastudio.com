What Is Pranayama?

Pranayama is a major discipline within the Indian Yoga tradition. It works with the breath through awareness, regulation and carefully developed practices.

In traditional Yoga, breath is closely connected with prana, a term used for vital energy or life-force. Different Yoga traditions explain prana in different ways.

Pranayama is therefore more than simply taking deep breaths. It is a disciplined approach to breathing that can become increasingly refined as a practitioner develops.

Beginners should first learn to observe their natural breath. More demanding practices should be introduced gradually and, where appropriate, under qualified guidance.

TRADITIONAL YOGA VIEW

Prana and the Breath

Traditional Yoga literature often describes prana as a fundamental life principle associated with living beings.

Breath is treated as an important doorway into the practice because it is both closely connected with the body and capable of being consciously observed.

The relationship between breath, attention and the mind is an important theme throughout many Yoga traditions.

Breath

The physical process of inhalation and exhalation that can be observed directly.

Prana

A traditional Yoga concept referring to vital energy or life-force.

Awareness

The foundation through which breathing becomes an object of conscious practice.

ESSENTIAL VOCABULARY

The Three Core Breath Actions

Puraka

Inhalation. The drawing of breath inward.

Rechaka

Exhalation. The releasing of breath outward.

Kumbhaka

Breath retention. Traditional Yoga describes internal and external forms of retention.

Retention is an advanced area of practice and should never be forced.

FOUNDATION

Breath Awareness Before Breath Control

A strong Pranayama foundation begins with observation.

Notice the natural rhythm of breathing without immediately trying to change it.

  • Observe the natural breath.
  • Notice whether breathing feels smooth or strained.
  • Relax unnecessary tension in the face and shoulders.
  • Allow the breath to remain comfortable.
  • Develop attention before attempting advanced regulation.

PREPARATION

Preparing for Pranayama

Posture

Sit in a stable and comfortable position. Keep the spine naturally upright without stiffness.

Environment

Choose a clean, quiet and reasonably comfortable place for practice.

Attention

Reduce distractions and give the practice your full attention.

Comfort

Breathing should remain comfortable. Avoid unnecessary force.

PRACTICE PANORAMA

Major Breath Practices

The following practices represent important methods found in Yoga teaching and literature. Exact instructions can vary between traditions and teachers.

Anulom Vilom

A widely taught alternate-nostril breathing practice. It is commonly introduced as a gentle method for developing awareness, rhythm and concentration.

Beginners should learn the technique slowly rather than trying to increase speed or breath retention.

Nadi Shodhana

A traditional alternate-nostril practice associated with purification and balancing concepts in Yoga.

Terminology and exact technique may differ between lineages. Some teachers use Anulom Vilom and Nadi Shodhana almost interchangeably, while others distinguish them.

Sama Vritti

A practice based on an even or balanced breathing pattern.

It can introduce beginners to conscious rhythm without requiring forceful breathing.

Bhramari

Commonly known as humming-bee breath because of its characteristic humming sound during exhalation.

It is often used as a calming and attention-oriented practice.

Ujjayi

A traditional breathing method often associated with a soft, controlled sound created through gentle regulation of the throat.

It is widely used in different Yoga traditions, including some forms of Hatha and modern Yoga practice.

Sheetali

A traditional cooling breath practice. Its method involves a specialized form of inhalation.

Technique should be learned correctly rather than copied from an incomplete description.

Sheetkari

Another traditional cooling-oriented breathing practice with a method distinct from Sheetali.

Surya Bhedana

A traditional pranayama involving a specific nostril-breathing pattern and associated with solar symbolism in Yoga traditions.

More structured versions should be learned from a qualified teacher.

Chandra Bhedana

A traditional nostril-breathing practice associated with lunar symbolism in Yoga traditions.

ADVANCED STUDY

Traditional Forceful Breathing Practices

Some Yoga traditions include stronger breathing methods such as Kapalabhati and Bhastrika.

These practices are different from simple breath awareness. They may involve stronger or faster respiratory actions.

They should not be treated as casual exercises or performed competitively.

Beginners should first establish a comfortable foundation and learn advanced methods through appropriate instruction.

Important

Strong breathing, prolonged retention and advanced techniques are not necessary for every practitioner. More intensity does not automatically mean better Yoga.

DEEPER STUDY

Kumbhaka: Breath Retention

Kumbhaka refers to breath retention in traditional Pranayama terminology.

Antara Kumbhaka

Retention after inhalation.

Bahya Kumbhaka

Retention after exhalation.

Traditional Pranayama systems may use structured relationships between inhalation, retention and exhalation.

Such practices require progressive learning. Breath retention should never be forced simply to achieve a longer duration.

SUBTLE-BODY TRADITIONS

Nadis in Yoga

Traditional Yoga and related Indian systems describe a subtle network known as nadis.

Three names appear prominently in many Yoga traditions:

Ida

Traditionally associated with lunar symbolism and described within subtle-body frameworks.

Pingala

Traditionally associated with solar symbolism and described within subtle-body frameworks.

Sushumna

Traditionally described as a central subtle channel in Yoga and Tantra traditions.

These are traditional subtle-body concepts. They should not be presented as if they were established physical anatomical structures.

YOGA CONNECTION

Pranayama and Meditation

Pranayama and meditation are closely connected in many Yoga traditions.

Breath awareness can provide a stable object for attention. Regulated breathing may also become part of preparation for deeper concentration and meditation practices.

Patanjali's classical Yoga framework places Pranayama after Asana and before Pratyahara, Dharana and Dhyana in the sequence of the eight limbs.

Pratyahara

Withdrawal or turning of attention away from habitual sensory engagement.

Dharana

Concentration or sustained attention.

Dhyana

Meditation or sustained meditative awareness.

FOR EVERY AGE

Pranayama for Different Age Groups

Children

Begin with simple breathing awareness, gentle observation and playful breath-based activities.

Strong breathwork and prolonged retention should not be treated as children's games.

Young People

Introduce breath awareness, simple rhythm practices, concentration and appropriate relaxation methods.

Adults

Adults can progressively explore foundational Pranayama, traditional methods and deeper study.

Seniors

Comfortable, gentle breathing practices may be more appropriate than forceful or demanding techniques.

Yoga Teachers

Teachers should understand technique, progression, communication, individual differences and responsible instruction.

PRACTICE WISDOM

Common Pranayama Mistakes

  • Trying to breathe as deeply as possible all the time.
  • Using unnecessary force.
  • Holding the breath for too long.
  • Increasing speed simply to make practice harder.
  • Ignoring discomfort.
  • Copying advanced techniques without learning their purpose.
  • Treating every traditional technique as suitable for every person.
  • Comparing breath capacity with another practitioner.
  • Practising difficult techniques without appropriate instruction.

RESPONSIBLE PRACTICE

Comfort, Awareness and Good Guidance Come First

Pranayama should be approached progressively. Breathing practices should never become a contest.

If a practice causes pain, significant distress, unusual breathlessness, dizziness or other concerning symptoms, stop the practice and seek appropriate professional advice.

People with relevant medical conditions, pregnancy, respiratory problems or other individual concerns should obtain suitable professional guidance before undertaking demanding breath practices.

This website provides educational information. It does not replace individual assessment or medical care.

TEACHER DEVELOPMENT

Teaching Pranayama Responsibly

Teaching breath practices requires more than knowing the names of techniques.

Start Simple

Establish natural breath awareness before introducing complex patterns.

Progress Gradually

Increase complexity only when the learner is ready.

Observe

Watch posture, comfort, attention and breathing quality.

Respect Lineages

Different Yoga traditions may teach the same practice with different terminology or methods.

Never Pressure

A student should never be pressured to hold the breath, breathe faster or practise beyond comfortable limits.

Teach the Purpose

Students benefit when they understand what a practice is, why it is being taught and how it fits into Yoga.

LEARNING PATH

A Progressive Pranayama Journey

01 — Awareness

Observe natural breathing and develop attention.

02 — Foundation

Learn comfortable breathing patterns and simple practices such as Sama Vritti.

03 — Alternate Nostril Practices

Study Anulom Vilom and Nadi Shodhana with proper attention to technique.

04 — Traditional Practices

Progress toward practices such as Bhramari, Ujjayi, Sheetali and Sheetkari.

05 — Deeper Study

Study Kumbhaka, classical terminology, Hatha Yoga sources and lineage-specific approaches.

06 — Teacher Study

Learn how to explain, demonstrate, adapt and teach Pranayama responsibly.

TRADITION AND MODERN LEARNING

Traditional Knowledge, Presented Responsibly

Ancient Indian Yoga contains many schools, texts and teaching lineages. There is no single Pranayama method that represents every Yoga tradition.

Ancient Indian Yoga Studio will therefore present practices with their traditional context wherever possible.

Modern scientific explanations may be discussed separately when appropriate. Traditional concepts should not be presented as established scientific facts.

Our goal is simple: preserve the depth of Yoga while making its knowledge easier to understand.
